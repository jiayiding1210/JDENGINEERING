REHABILITATION & RETROFITTING

Extend service life with targeted intervention

Rehabilitation and retrofitting aim to recover performance, increase capacity, improve resilience, or slow deterioration without unnecessary replacement. The most effective intervention begins with understanding the governing deficiency, how the structure actually behaves, and how a repair can restore system-level performance in a durable and constructible way.

INTERVENTION FRAMEWORK

Repair, strengthen, and adapt with purpose

Rehabilitation should connect condition assessment, structural analysis, repair objectives, constructability, durability, and future maintenance. Strengthening a component without addressing the deterioration mechanism, load path, drainage, detailing, or adjacent elements can simply move the problem elsewhere. In practice, the strongest rehabilitation strategies are the ones that restore structural behavior while remaining buildable under real traffic, access, and budget constraints.

01

Assessment before intervention

Establish deterioration extent, residual capacity, active mechanisms, boundary conditions, and governing limit states before selecting a repair or strengthening strategy.

02

Concrete strengthening

Approaches may include section restoration, externally bonded or near-surface-mounted FRP, supplemental reinforcement, post-tensioning, jacketing, crack injection, overlays, and localized replacement.

03

Steel strengthening

Plate or member strengthening, bolted or welded retrofit details, fatigue-detail improvement, connection modification, section-loss repair, and load-path enhancement require close attention to fatigue, residual stress, fit-up, and erection sequence.

04

Durable material systems

UHPC, FRP, stainless steel hardware, improved coatings, and optimized cementitious materials are increasingly used where bond, corrosion resistance, rapid return to service, and long-term durability are critical.

05

Seismic & multihazard retrofit

Isolation, restrainers, column or foundation strengthening, ductility enhancement, seat extensions, bearing replacement, and load-path improvements can reduce vulnerability to seismic and other extreme demands.

06

Construction staging & traffic

Temporary support, jacking, load transfer, partial demolition, sequence-dependent behavior, access, curing, and maintenance of traffic can govern the feasibility of an otherwise sound retrofit concept.

RESEARCH EXAMPLE

Prestressed concrete slab beam bridge rehabilitation

Jiayi’s doctoral research investigated a recurring deficiency in prestressed concrete slab beam bridges: deterioration of longitudinal shear keys and the resulting loss of transverse load distribution. Rather than treating reflective cracking as an isolated surface defect, the study approached the problem as a loss of system-level continuity.

The rehabilitation method combines near-surface-mounted stainless-steel dowels in epoxy-filled grooves with externally bonded CFRP laminates. The system is intended to bypass deteriorated joints, restore transverse load transfer, and keep most construction activity beneath the bridge to reduce traffic disruption.

WHY THIS MATTERS

System-level deficiencies often require system-level solutions

In adjacent slab beam systems, deterioration at longitudinal shear keys can significantly reduce transverse load sharing. When that happens, individual beams attract more demand, cracking becomes more pronounced, serviceability worsens, and rating assumptions may become increasingly uncertain. A successful rehabilitation therefore must do more than patch cracks—it must re-establish how the bridge works as a system.

RESEARCH WORKFLOW

From observed deterioration to field-validated rehabilitation

The study progressed from understanding the recurring structural deficiency to developing, analyzing, testing, implementing, and evaluating a practical rehabilitation method.

01

Condition & mechanism

Characterize shear-key deterioration, loss of transverse load sharing, corrosion pathways, and system-level consequences.

02

Rehabilitation concept

Develop an NSM stainless-steel dowel and CFRP system intended to restore continuity between adjacent slab beams.

03

Numerical analysis

Use finite-element models to study live-load distribution, refine detailing, and compare pre- and post-rehabilitation response.

04

Laboratory validation

Evaluate tensile and shear resistance, bond behavior, confinement, and governing failure modes of the rehabilitation detail.

05

Diagnostic load testing

Measure in-service structural response before and after rehabilitation to quantify changes in system behavior and load distribution.

06

Field implementation & decision framework

Translate the method into constructible procedures and integrate performance, lifecycle cost, traffic impact, and feasibility into rehabilitation-versus-replacement decisions.

KEY IDEAS FROM THE RESEARCH

What this rehabilitation approach demonstrates

  • Rehabilitation should target behavior, not only damage. The central objective is to restore load-sharing, stiffness compatibility, and system-level continuity, not simply cover visible cracks.
  • Durable details matter. Stainless steel reinforcement, epoxy confinement, and CFRP strengthening can be combined to address both structural demands and long-term durability concerns.
  • Validation improves confidence. Finite element analysis, laboratory testing, and diagnostic load testing create a strong basis for design refinement and for demonstrating effectiveness to owners.
  • Constructability is part of the design. Details that can be installed from below the bridge, with limited demolition and reduced lane closures, are often more practical for in-service structures.
  • Rehabilitation and replacement should be evaluated strategically. Structural condition, load capacity, remaining service life, life-cycle cost, traffic impacts, and feasibility should all inform the final decision.

APPLICATIONS & CONSIDERATIONS

Ideas that extend beyond one bridge type

The research highlights a broader rehabilitation philosophy relevant to many bridge systems: use targeted strengthening to recover structural behavior, verify assumptions with measurement, and align intervention details with field constraints. Similar ideas are increasingly valuable in rehabilitation of adjacent precast systems, deteriorated deck-and-girder bridges, and other aging infrastructure where agencies need to extend service life without immediate replacement.

In practical application, engineers should evaluate whether a proposed rehabilitation addresses the active deterioration mechanism, whether the existing structure can still provide a reliable substrate for strengthening, how loads will redistribute after intervention, and what inspection or maintenance strategy will be required over the extended service life.
