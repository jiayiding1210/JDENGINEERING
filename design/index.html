STRUCTURAL DESIGN

Designing for performance, constructability, and service life

Structural design is the integration of mechanics, materials, detailing, construction, durability, and lifecycle performance. A strong design is not only code-compliant; it is buildable, inspectable, maintainable, and appropriate for its environment.

DESIGN FRAMEWORK

From structural concept to durable infrastructure

Modern bridge design increasingly begins with system-level decisions—structural form, span arrangement, materials, foundations, construction method, durability strategy, and digital delivery—before detailed member sizing. Those early choices often control lifecycle performance more than isolated calculations.

01

Structural system selection

Choose structural form and materials by balancing span requirements, geometry, redundancy, stiffness, fabrication, erection, inspection access, maintenance, and lifecycle cost.

02

Concrete, steel & hybrid systems

Prestressed concrete, post-tensioned segmental construction, steel plate or tub girders, composite systems, and hybrid solutions each require distinct treatment of durability, fatigue, stability, detailing, and construction sequence.

03

Substructures & foundations

Pier geometry, cap behavior, deep foundations, soil interaction, scour, vessel impact, and seismic demands should be integrated with superstructure behavior rather than treated as isolated components.

04

Model-based & parametric design

Parametric geometry and information-rich 3D models can reduce repetitive modeling, improve multidisciplinary coordination, support automated checks, and carry information forward into fabrication, construction, inspection, and asset management.

05

Accelerated construction

Prefabricated bridge elements and systems, modularization, rapid connections, and staged construction can reduce traffic disruption and field exposure when the design accounts for transportation, erection, tolerances, connections, and temporary conditions.

06

Service-life design

Durability is increasingly treated explicitly through exposure assessment, material selection, detailing, corrosion protection, drainage, joints, replaceable components, and strategies that reduce future access and intervention needs.

DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS

Design decisions extend beyond strength

  • Constructability from the beginning. Erection sequence, crane access, temporary stability, tolerances, formwork, falsework, traffic maintenance, and fabrication constraints should influence the concept before final detailing.
  • Detail for durability. Water management, drainage, joint strategy, reinforcement congestion, crack control, corrosion exposure, fatigue-prone details, and inspection access directly affect service life.
  • Design the interfaces. Connections between structural systems, disciplines, precast elements, utilities, and existing infrastructure are frequent sources of risk.
  • Account for uncertainty. Material variability, soil behavior, future traffic, environmental exposure, and evolving use require robust—not merely optimized—solutions.
  • Preserve engineering transparency. Automation and parametric tools should make assumptions and checks more traceable, not obscure the basis of design.

CURRENT DIRECTION

Digital delivery and lifecycle thinking are reshaping design

The industry is moving toward model-based delivery, reusable digital information, parametric workflows, accelerated construction, and explicit service-life design. These approaches can reduce rework and traffic impacts while improving coordination and long-term performance when digital models remain connected to sound structural mechanics and constructability.
